A DC-DC converter is an electronic circuit that converts a source of direct current from one voltage level to another. Switching converters store energy in an inductor or capacitor and release it at a different voltage, achieving efficiencies above ninety percent. The buck converter steps voltage down by chopping the input with a transistor and filtering the result. The boost converter steps voltage up by charging an inductor and releasing its stored energy in series with the input. A buck-boost topology can produce an output above or below the input. Control loops regulate the duty cycle to hold the output constant against load and line changes. Synchronous rectification replaces the freewheeling diode with a second transistor to reduce conduction loss. Higher switching frequencies shrink the passive components but raise switching loss, so designers trade size against efficiency. Gallium nitride transistors switch faster than silicon and permit smaller inductors at the same ripple. Layout matters because the high di/dt loop must be kept physically small to limit radiated emissions.
